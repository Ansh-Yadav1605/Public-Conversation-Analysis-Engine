Problem Statement

Product teams trying to understand why users hesitate, stall, or abandon a specific behavior — like adding a fashion product to a wishlist but never buying it — routinely default to internal analytics and gut-instinct hypotheses, because reading real user language at scale across App Store reviews, Play Store reviews, Reddit, shopping communities, social media, YouTube comments, and product Q&A is too slow and manual to do consistently. This means product decisions get made on assumption rather than evidence, and the qualitative research that could catch a wrong assumption tends to arrive late — as validation of a solution someone already committed to, not as early discovery of the real problem.

Compounding this, public conversation data is messy and inconsistent across sources: a single review or thread can contain multiple distinct signals — a fit complaint, a styling doubt, a price hesitation, a trust concern — with no shared structure to make them comparable, clusterable, or rankable. Sentiment analysis alone can't fix this, since two "negative" mentions can point to completely different root causes.

What this engine does: scrapes and analyzes public conversation across App Store reviews, Play Store reviews, Reddit discussions, fashion and shopping communities, social media, YouTube comments, and product reviews/Q&A, to answer a defined set of behavioral questions that summarization or sentiment analysis alone cannot:

1. Why do users add fashion products to their wishlist in the first place?
2. What prevents wishlisted products from eventually being purchased?
3. What uncertainties remain after a user has already identified a product they like?
4. What causes users to postpone a purchase rather than abandon or complete it?
5. How do users compare multiple shortlisted products against each other?
6. What information do users seek outside the platform (Myntra/AJIO and beyond) before deciding?
7. What role do fit, size, styling, price, reviews, occasion, and social validation each play — and how do they interact?
8. When is a wishlist add genuine purchase intent, and when is it just bookmarking?
9. How do these behaviors and motivations differ across user segments?
10. What unmet needs show up consistently, across independent conversations, rather than as one-off complaints?

The engine goes beyond summarizing or scoring sentiment: it identifies discrete opportunity areas, quantifies them where evidence allows (frequency, severity, segment concentration), and makes them comparable against each other — so a product team can move from "we don't know why users behave this way" to a ranked, evidence-tagged shortlist worth taking into primary research.

Why it's built generically: while this brief and the graduation project both anchor on fashion e-commerce, the underlying pipeline — source scraping, taxonomy-based extraction, opportunity clustering and scoring — isn't fashion-specific. The taxonomy and question set are configurable inputs, not hardcoded logic, so the same engine could be re-pointed at a different product category or business problem without a rebuild.

What it explicitly is not: a replacement for primary user research (interviews remain the validation layer, per this project's own research sequence), and not a sentiment-analysis or review-summarization tool — those are explicitly named as insufficient for the goal.

Success looks like: given the fashion-shopping source list above and this question set, the engine returns a structured, source-traceable set of opportunity areas — each with frequency/severity/evidence-strength where the data supports it — answering the ten questions above with real evidence rather than assumption, in far less time than a manual research pass would take.
